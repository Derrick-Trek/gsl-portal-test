HLA 1 addresses the high-leverage action of co-creating a supportive culture of high expectations. This document will help you understand the GSL curriculum for HLA 1 so that you can adapt it to your needs; contextualize it with appropriate examples, local terminology, and relevant case studies; and finally, implement it confidently!

Before you review the HLA 1 curriculum, please read the HLA 1 introduction. This will help you understand what co-creating a supportive culture of high expectations looks like. If you would also like to understand the research underpinning this curriculum, please read the Evidence and Key Studies section, which contains evidence and key studies about the critical role of culture, relationships, beliefs, and expectations.

About the HLA 1 Curriculum Sequence

This curriculum is arranged into ten training sessions, each lasting up to two hours. All sessions are written to work as both virtual and in-person sessions.

The curriculum can be used in different ways.

  • As bite-sized content: You may want to go through the sessions, and identify activities or parts of sessions that you can integrate into your existing approaches.

  • As single training sessions embedded within your current curriculum. You may want to use whole sessions (with some contextualization).

  • As a complete curriculum to be delivered over up to nine workshops.

How to help participants implement what they learn in the training sessions

These training sessions are likely to be most effective when supplemented with

  • Coaching or 1:1 support sessions to help participants reflect on and implement what they are learning.

  • Learning communities to help participants share their experiences with one another.

  • Assignments to help participants explore and implement what they are learning.

    • Each training session ends with an assignment to help participants practice the session objectives.

    • The Practitioner’s Guide contains six job-embedded assignments for school leaders and teachers to practice specific skills. Each assignment contains instructions for the school leader or teacher and guidance for coaches supporting school leaders through the assignment.

How did we decide what to include and emphasize in this curriculum?

This curriculum is based on research into the role of relationships, beliefs, and behaviors in shaping a culture that allows students to succeed. The research base is documented in the Research and Resource Guide. As you can imagine, with such a vast amount of information available about school culture, we had to choose what to include, what to exclude, and what to emphasize when designing this curriculum. Here are some of the critical choices we made for your reference - and for you to consider, too, as you adapt and implement.

1. We chose to spend a lot of time making the case that culture is an important part of school leadership. We spent a whole session (E1) exploring the idea of culture and breaking it down into beliefs, norms, and visible behaviors. This is because ‘culture’ is an abstract term and might be seen as something that just exists and cannot be shaped by school leaders. We felt that investing time in an introduction to culture would make it seem more concrete. Similarly, we dedicated a full session (E5) for school leadership to consider how to engage teachers in building school culture.

2. We emphasized teacher beliefs and expectations. We felt that an entire session (E2) on high expectations and teacher beliefs was merited because teacher (and school leader) beliefs are often unexplored, sometimes unhelpful, and always influential. If school culture and instructional processes are to improve, it all starts with beliefs about students, what they are capable of, and what they deserve from school.

3. We focused on classroom culture, in one session that looked at general aspects of positive classroom culture (P1) and another that explores positive behavior management as an alternative to negative reactions and corporal punishment (P2). We felt that it was important to look at teacher-student relationships and behavior management because, in many settings, teachers and school leaders do not have adequate opportunities to reflect on their behavior management practices. We also included socioemotional learning as something that can be integrated into classroom culture, since there is increasing understanding that students’ socioemotional learning can be supported deliberately at school.

4. Towards the end of the series of sessions, we include a session on sustaining changes in school culture because school culture work is never finished; it is an ongoing endeavor for school leaders as staff and students join and leave the school and as the school goes through different phases. Therefore we wanted to make some suggestions to school leaders about how they can monitor and continue to support school culture. The very last session (D2) looks at how we can incorporate families in building school culture since family support is critical for the success of schools, and families are often overlooked when we consider questions of school improvement.
